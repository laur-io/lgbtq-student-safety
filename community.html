Community-based support and alternatives

Safety can come from community

Safety and support do not have to come from formal systems.

Building a supportive community can help LGBTQ+ students feel less isolated when dealing with harassment or discrimination. This can be as simple as listening without judging, respecting someone's privacy, helping them find resources, or creating spaces where people feel comfortable being themselves.

How to support a friend

One of the most important things you can do is listen to a friend without judgment and respect their decisions. It's best to avoid pressuring them into reporting or making them share information they are not comfortable sharing.

Instead, ask what they need from you, help them explore their options, and offer to help them make a report if they choose to.

Bystander intervention

If you witness harm, harassment, or discrimination, there are several ways you can help. You could speak up when it is safe to do so, interrupt the situation, or simply check in with the person afterward.

You do not always have to confront someone directly to make a difference. Consider the safety of the person facing harm as well as your own.

Community accountability

Creating a safe community also means holding ourselves and those around us accountable for our actions. If a friend makes a discriminatory comment or engages directly in harmful behavior, you can challenge their ideas, try to educate them, and encourage them to understand the impact of their words or actions.

Community accountability focuses on changing harmful behavior rather than ignoring the issue or relying on institutional punishment.

Transformative justice

Transformative justice is an approach to addressing harm that focuses on healing, accountability, and preventing future harm. It encourages communities to examine why harm occurs and what can be done to make long-lasting change.

This approach centers the safety, needs, and choices of the person who experienced harm.

Mutual aid and student organizing

Students can support one another by sharing resources, creating peer support networks, organizing LGBTQ+ student groups, and advocating for change within their campus community.

Mutual aid is based on the idea that people can work together to meet each other's needs rather than relying on institutions. Student organizing can help address larger issues that contribute to harassment on campus.

Formal punishment is only one possible response

Not all students feel safe or comfortable relying on campus police or reporting systems, especially those belonging to historically marginalized communities. Formal reporting and punishment are only some of the possible responses to harm.

Students can seek support through trusted friends, community support, confidential counseling, peer networks, and off-campus resources. Students should be able to explore their options without feeling pressured into a particular response.

Further reading

  • Kelley, R. D. G. (2016). Black Study, Black Struggle . Kelley's work encourages us to think about the role of collective action, solidarity, and community in challenging systems of inequality.
  • Spade, D. (2020). Mutual Aid: Building Solidarity During This Crisis (and the Next). Verso. Spade explores how communities can work together to meet people's needs while addressing systems of inequality and harm. It connects directly to peer support and organizing.
  • Piepzna-Samarasinha, L. L. (2018). Care Work: Dreaming Disability Justice. Arsenal Pulp Press. This explores how marginalized communities create networks of care and support, particularly when existing institutions fail to meet their needs.
  • Stanley, E. A., & Smith, N. (Eds.). (2015). Captive Genders: Trans Embodiment and the Prison Industrial Complex (2nd ed.). AK Press. This helps us understand why LGBTQ+ individuals may not feel safe relying on punishment-based systems.
  • Boggs, A., Meyerhoff, E., Mitchell, N., & Schwartz-Weinstein, Z. (2019). Abolitionist University Studies: An invitation. Abolition University. Their work on abolitionist approaches to universities encourages us to question how university systems operate and to imagine alternative ways of creating safety, support, and accountability within campus communities.
